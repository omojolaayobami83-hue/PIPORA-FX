LESSON 01 · BEGINNER

Market Structure

Learn how swing highs and swing lows help describe the market's direction.

Tap to show or hide lesson notes

1. Higher High (HH)

A higher high forms when a new swing high rises above the previous swing high.

Note: Repeated higher highs can show that buyers are pushing price upward. Do not call every tiny candle peak a swing high; wait for a clear turning point.

2. Higher Low (HL)

A higher low forms when price pulls back but its next swing low stays above the previous swing low.

Note: In an uptrend, buyers often step in before price falls to the previous low. A higher low can support a bullish view, but it is not a guarantee that price will rise.

3. Lower High (LH)

A lower high forms when price rises, turns down, and fails to reach the previous swing high.

Note: Lower highs can show that buying pressure is weakening and sellers are becoming more active. Look for confirmation rather than selling only because one lower high appears.

4. Lower Low (LL)

A lower low forms when a new swing low falls below the previous swing low.

Note: Repeated lower lows can describe a downtrend. A single dip below a low may be a liquidity sweep, so check whether price closes beyond the level and what happens next.

5. Bullish and bearish structure

A sequence of HHs and HLs describes bullish structure. A sequence of LHs and LLs describes bearish structure.

Note: Markets can move sideways or change direction. Identify the timeframe you are studying and avoid mixing a 5-minute structure with a 4-hour conclusion.
Visual guide · UptrendSchematic only: rising swing highs and rising swing lows.
Practice: Open a chart, mark two clear swing highs and two clear swing lows, then decide whether the sequence is bullish, bearish, or ranging. If the swings are unclear, write “uncertain”.
LESSON 02 · BEGINNER

Support & Resistance

Understand price areas where buying or selling has previously appeared.

Tap to show or hide lesson notes

1. Support

Support is a price area where falling price has previously slowed or bounced as buyers became more active.

Note: Support is an area, not an exact magic line. The more clearly price reacts there, the more useful the area may be to watch, but it can still break.

2. Resistance

Resistance is a price area where rising price has previously slowed or turned down as sellers became more active.

Note: Mark the zone around repeated reactions instead of trying to place one perfect line. Resistance can fail when buying pressure is strong.

3. How to mark key levels

Look left on the chart for clear swing highs, swing lows, and areas where price reacted more than once.

Note: Start with the 1-hour or 4-hour chart for broader levels, then use a lower timeframe for detail. Avoid filling the chart with too many lines.

4. How price reacts

Price may reject a zone, pause around it, or break through and later return to test it.

Note: Wait for a candle close and follow-up behaviour. A wick through a zone alone does not prove a breakout or reversal.

5. Role reversal

After a decisive break, old resistance may act as support, or old support may act as resistance.

Note: This is sometimes called a retest. It does not happen every time; price can continue without returning to the level.
Visual guide · ZonesZones are areas of interest, not guaranteed turning points.
Practice: Mark one support zone and one resistance zone on Gold or GBPUSD. Observe how price behaves when it reaches each zone; do not enter a trade just because price touches it.
LESSON 03 · INTERMEDIATE

Break of Structure (BOS)

Learn what it means when price breaks an important previous swing.

Tap to show or hide lesson notes

1. Bullish BOS

A bullish break of structure occurs when price decisively breaks above a relevant previous swing high in a bullish context.

Note: Many traders look for a candle close above the swing high rather than a wick alone. The level and timeframe matter.

2. Bearish BOS

A bearish break of structure occurs when price decisively breaks below a relevant previous swing low in a bearish context.

Note: A quick move below a low that immediately reverses may be a sweep rather than a sustained break. Look at the close and subsequent candles.

3. Change of Character (CHOCH)

CHOCH is a term traders use for an early break against the previous market direction, which may warn that behaviour is changing.

Note: It is an early warning, not proof of a new trend. Wait for follow-through and clearer structure before treating a reversal as established.

4. Confirming a breakout

Check whether price closes beyond the level, whether the move has follow-through, and whether the broader timeframe agrees.

Note: Avoid chasing a candle after a large move. A retest can happen, but it is not guaranteed. Always plan invalidation and risk before any trade.
Visual guide · Break of structureLook for a meaningful close beyond the swing level and follow-through.
Practice: Find a clear swing high and swing low. Replay the chart candle by candle and note whether price broke the level with a close, only wicked through it, or failed to break.
LESSON 04 · INTERMEDIATE

Liquidity

Understand areas where stop orders may gather and why price can move through them.

Tap to show or hide lesson notes

1. Buy-side liquidity (BSL)

Buy-side liquidity is commonly discussed around highs where traders may place buy-stop orders, including stops from short positions.

Note: Equal highs and obvious swing highs can attract attention, but you cannot see every order from a normal chart. Treat these as possible liquidity areas, not certain targets.

2. Sell-side liquidity (SSL)

Sell-side liquidity is commonly discussed around lows where sell-stop orders may gather, including stops from long positions.

Note: Equal lows and obvious swing lows are areas traders watch. A move through them does not automatically mean price must reverse.

3. Liquidity sweep

A sweep happens when price trades beyond a watched high or low and then moves back inside the level or zone.

Note: Look for the reaction after the sweep, such as a close back inside and a structure shift. A sweep can also continue into a genuine breakout.

4. Stop hunts

“Stop hunt” is a popular trading phrase for a move through an obvious level that triggers stop orders before price reverses or continues.

Note: A chart alone cannot prove that a broker or institution deliberately hunted your stop. Focus on observable price behaviour instead of assuming intent.

5. What to check after a sweep

Watch whether price returns inside the level, forms a clear rejection, and then breaks a nearby minor swing in the opposite direction.

Note: This combination may provide context, not certainty. If there is no clear confirmation, waiting is a valid decision.

Practice: Mark equal highs or equal lows on a chart. Watch what happens when price reaches them. Record whether it rejects, closes beyond, or stays undecided.
